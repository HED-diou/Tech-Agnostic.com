Where the cloud bill actually goes

Most teams cannot attribute more than 60% of their cloud spend. The missing 40% is where the savings are, and finding it takes about a day.

Tech-Agnostic2 May 20266 min read

Ask an engineering leader what their cloud bill is and you will get an accurate number. Ask them what it is for and the answer thins out fast.

In our experience most teams can confidently attribute somewhere between 50% and 60% of spend. The rest is a long tail of things nobody has looked at since the person who created them left.

That untagged remainder is not evenly distributed waste. It is where nearly all of the recoverable money sits.

Attribution before optimisation

The instinct is to start cutting: resize the instances, buy the reserved capacity, turn on the storage lifecycle rules. Do not start there. You will save something, annoy people, and have no way to prove which change did what.

Start by making every euro attributable to one of four buckets:

  • Serving — capacity that handles live user traffic.
  • Batch — scheduled or asynchronous work.
  • Environments — staging, preview, QA, demo.
  • Orphaned — attached to nothing that is still running.

You do not need a FinOps platform to do this. A tagging policy, one afternoon with the billing export, and a spreadsheet gets you 90% of the way.

The four things we find every time

After roughly thirty of these exercises, the findings are boringly consistent.

1. Always-on capacity doing batch work

A cluster provisioned for peak, running nightly jobs at 3 a.m. and idling for the other 21 hours. This is usually the single largest line item, and it is almost always the easiest to fix, because scheduled capacity has no user-facing risk.

2. Environments nobody uses

Preview environments that spin up per pull request and never spin down. Staging sized identically to production “so the tests are realistic”, running at 4% CPU. Check the last login timestamp on each one before you defend it.

3. Storage without a lifecycle

Logs, backups and build artefacts retained forever because nobody chose a retention period. This one grows quietly and compounds, and the fix is a policy rather than a project.

4. Egress from an architecture decision made three years ago

Cross-region or cross-AZ traffic generated by a topology that made sense when it was chosen and does not now. Hardest to fix, and worth measuring even if you decide not to.

Guardrails, not a one-off cleanup

A cost cleanup that is not followed by guardrails reverts within about two quarters. We have watched it happen. Before you close the project:

  • Put budget alerts on each bucket, not on the account total. An account total tells you something went wrong a month after it did.
  • Make the tagging policy enforceable in the infrastructure-as-code layer. Untagged resources should fail the plan, not generate a ticket.
  • Put cost per customer, or cost per unit of whatever you sell, on the same dashboard as latency and errors. It is a reliability metric, and it belongs where engineers already look.

What “good” looks like

You are done when someone can answer, in under a minute and without asking anyone: what did serving cost last month, and how does that compare to revenue?

Every team we have worked with could answer that question within three weeks of starting. None of them could answer it on day one.
